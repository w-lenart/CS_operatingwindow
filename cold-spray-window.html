Velocity against particle size

Impact and critical velocity both fall with size, at different rates. They cross at the largest size that still bonds.

What the model does

  1. Solves one-dimensional isentropic flow through a converging-diverging nozzle for the gas you select, giving local Mach number, temperature, pressure and density along the divergent section from the area ratio you enter.
  2. Integrates the drag force balance v_p dv_p/dz = (3/4) C_d ρ (v−v_p)|v−v_p| / (ρ_p d_p) along that section, with a constant drag coefficient, to get particle velocity at the nozzle exit.
  3. Integrates particle temperature over the same path using lumped capacitance with a Ranz-Marshall Nusselt number, started at throat conditions to approximate upstream powder injection. Impact temperature matters because it lowers the critical velocity.
  4. Evaluates critical velocity from the Schmidt-Gärtner-Assadi correlation v_cr = 667 − 14ρ + 0.08T_m + 0.1σ_u − 0.4T_i with a size correction proportional to d^−0.18 normalised at 25 µm.
  5. Reports η, the ratio of impact to critical velocity. Coating characteristics have been shown to collapse onto a single function of η, which is what makes a temperature-pressure map with η contours a usable parameter selection tool.

A check you can run

Select copper at 25 µm and drag the temperature down until particle impact temperature is near ambient. Critical velocity converges on roughly 640 m/s, which is the value measured by laser two-focus velocimetry for copper at Sandia, where deposition efficiencies reached 95 percent above it. An independently measured number falling out of the correlation is the reason to trust the rest of the chain.

Limits you should state before anyone finds them

  • The critical velocity correlation is a small-perturbation expression anchored on copper. It degrades as material properties move away from copper, so treat titanium, nickel alloys and especially the refractory presets as indicative only.
  • No bow shock. Impact velocity is taken as nozzle exit velocity, which measurement supports at ordinary standoff but which fails at short standoff or with very fine powder. The fines cutoff is a crude stand-in for particles lost to the shock and the plume; it is a dial, not a prediction.
  • The bonding band has two edges and they are not equally trustworthy. The fine edge emerges from the physics: small particles track the cooling gas, stay cold, and so carry a high critical velocity they cannot quite reach. The coarse edge runs optimistic, because drag coefficient is held constant and the critical velocity correlation is being extrapolated to impact temperatures well beyond where it was fitted. Read a coarse edge reported above the plotted range as not velocity-limited here, not as everything sticks.
  • The mass-in-band figure is an upper bound on deposition efficiency, taken as the mass fraction of a lognormal size distribution lying inside the band. At a well-chosen operating point it saturates near 100 percent, which is consistent with measured efficiencies for copper but means the discriminating information is the band edges rather than the percentage. It ignores tamping, re-erosion and in-flight oxidation.
  • No nozzle wall friction, no shock structure in the divergent section, no gas-particle loading effect, no atmospheric entrainment in the free jet. All four cost real velocity, so the model runs optimistic.
  • Gas transport properties are single representative values, not temperature-dependent.
